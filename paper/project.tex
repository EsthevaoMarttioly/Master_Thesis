% Fiscal transfers have been an important public policy instrument in various governments.
% Among the main examples, we can cite: \textit{Bolsa Família} (Brazil),
% \textit{Asignación Universal por Hijo} (Argentina), \textit{Progresa/Oportunidades} (Mexico),
% and \textit{Universal Credit} (United Kingdom). Although each program has its specificities,
% they all share the same objective: income redistribution.

% Whether a fiscal policy is targeted or universal makes all the difference when the issue is
% social justice. In standard macroeconomic models, all individuals are equals, meaning that
% transfers affect everyone in the same way \parencite{blanchard2007real}.

% However, households differ dramatically in their income, wealth, employment status, and propensity to
% consume. Microdata show that individuals have different marginal propensities to consume (MPC) depending
% on their income and credit constraints \parencite{kaplan2014model}.

% Therefore, a heterogeneous agent model is crucial to understand the impacts and differences between
% targeted and universal fiscal policies. In Heterogeneous Agent New-Keynesian (HANK) models, these differences
% matter a lot: the same transfer budget produces very different aggregate and distributional outcomes
% depending on targeting \parencite{auclert2025fiscal,kaplan2014model}.

% Literature has studied the effects of police targeting primarily in relation to police funding: whether
% through debt or taxes \parencite{mckay2016role}. Some other articles have studied the presence of fiscal
% transfers and unemployment insurance, mostly in isolation \parencite{krueger2016distribution,bartal2024consumption}.

% The idea behind this project is to systematically compare the effects of different fiscal policies to
% a universal lump-sum transfer, in the presence of a negative shock to aggregate demand that triggers
% the transfers. To answer this, I aim to simulate different fiscal structures, such as transfers based
% on wealth, income, employment status, or hand-to-mouth status, in a structural HANK model with unemployment.


%---------------------------------------------------
%	MODEL
%---------------------------------------------------
\section{Model}

The setup is a standard New Keynesian model with incomplete markets and labor
supply risk. Time is discrete and runs from $t=0$ to infinity. There are four
types of agents in the economy: households, firms, labor unions, and the government.

Households are heterogeneous \parencite{aiyagari1994uninsured}, workers and
firms join in a formal-informal setup, and the rest of the economy follows
the textbook New Keynesian model \parencite{gali2015monetary}. There is no
aggregate uncertainty and the economy starts in the deterministic steady-state.

%---------------------------------------------------
%	HOUSEHOLDS
\subsubsection*{Households}
Assume there exists a continuum $i\in[0,1]$ of ex-ante identical households,
but experiencing idiosyncratic shocks in labor productivity and discount factor.

Households enjoy a separable CRRA utility over consumption $c_{i,t}$ and worked
hours $h_{i,t}$, which we define $u(c_{i,t}, v_{i,t}) =  u(c_{i,t}) - v(h_{i,t})$ as
\[
\max_{c_{i,t},h_{i,t}} \; \E_0 \sum_{t=0}^\infty \beta^t \left[
\frac{c_{i,t}^{1-\frac{1}{\gamma}}}{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma}}
- \psi \frac{h_{i,t}^{1+\frac{1}{\varphi}}}{1+\frac{1}{\varphi}}
\right],
\]
where $\gamma$ is the elasticity of intertemporal substitution
(EIS) and $\varphi$ is the Frisch elasticity of labor supply.

A household faces idiosyncratic labor productivity shocks $e_{i,t}$ with
$\E[e_{i,t}] = 1$, and discount factor heterogeneity
$\beta_i\in\{ \beta_L, \beta_H \}$, and we fix $\omega_I$
impatient households. They can work in the formal or informal sector,
or be unemployed $s\in\{F,I,U\}$. Each sector has a specific productivity
$\theta^s_{i,t}$ which their wage depends on, re-drawn on each new offer,
with $\E\!\left[\theta^F_{i,t}\right] = 1$.

Formal workers earn labor income
$(1-\tau_{\ell}) w_t h_{i,t} e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t}^F$, while informal workers
earn $w_t h_{i,t} e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t}^I$, where $w_t$ is the baseline wage,
$\tau_{\ell}$ is a fixed labor income tax rate. Let $y_t(s,e,\theta)$ be this
non-financial income,
\[
    y_t(s,e,\theta) = \begin{cases}
        (1-\tau_\ell) w_t h_t e \theta^F, &\text{if formal;} \\
        w_t h_t e \theta^I, \quad &\text{if informal;} \\
        0, &\text{if unemployed}.
    \end{cases}
\]

Let $b_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ be the \textit{Bolsa Família} (BF) status, so that the
transfer size is $T_{i,t} = T_t b_{i,t}$. At the end of each period, non-recipients
in informality or unemployment enter with probability $\lambda$ if eligible by an
income criterium $y_{i,t} + r_t^a a_{i,t} < \bar y$. At the same time, beneficiaries
face a probability $\rho_s$ of being audited, where $\rho_F > \rho_I$.

We assume that audited formal recipients always leave, while informal and unemployed
households stay only if eligible. Formal income is cross-checked automatically,
however, the lag between entering a formal job and losing the benefit motivates
a $\rho_F < 1$. Therefore, the probability of being a recipient next period is
\[
\P\left[b'=1 | s,b,\theta,e,a \right] = q(s,b,\theta,e,a)
= \begin{cases}
    \lambda \cdot \mathbbm{1}\{ s \neq F, y + r a < \bar y \}, &\text{if } b=0 \\
    (1-\rho_s) + \rho_s \mathbbm{1}\{ s \neq F, y + r a < \bar y \}, &\text{if } b=1
\end{cases}.
\]

However, we assume that assets are hidden from the government, so the agency
approves within a group of observationally identical households, considering
their productivities $x = (\theta, e)$. Thus, the agency approves with a
conditional probability $\bar Q(x,b)$, weighted by the assets distribution,
given by
\[
\bar Q(x,b) = \E\left[ q(x,b,a) | x \right].
\]

Households save into a mutual fund of liquid assets $a_{i,t}$ with ex-post
real return $1+r_t^a$. They also receive lump-sum transfers $\tau_t$ by the
government. Their budget constraint at time $t$ reads:
\[
c_{i,t} + a_{i,t} = (1+r_t^a) a_{i,t-1} + y_{i,t}(s,e,\theta) + T_t b_{i,t} + \tau_t.
\]

Incomplete markets impose a borrowing constraint $a_{i,t} \geq \underline{a}$.
Frictions in the labor market imply formal households delegate their labor supply
decisions to labor unions, and thus take worked hours as given, so their labor
disutility is internalized at the union level, not by individual households.
While, informal workers choose hours to maximize their utility, thus, for each $i$,
the Intratemporal Condition gives us
% this is novel, better to explain it later
\[
\psi h^{1/\varphi} = w e \theta^I u'(c)
\quad \Leftrightarrow \quad
h^I(c,e,\theta) = \left[ \frac{w e \theta^I c^{-1/\gamma}}{\psi} \right]^\varphi.
\]

In order to avoid high-productivity workers going to informality to work
unrealistic hours, we impose a rigidity on informals hours, where informal
hours are capped at the formal full time $h^F$, so that
$h_{i,t}^I = \min\left\{ h^I(c,e,\theta), h^F \right\}$. This is motivated
by Figure \ref{fig:hours_wage}.

Such as \textcite{esteban2021labor} and \textcite{finamor2025labor},
given a sector $s$, we assume that each worker faces an exogenous
layoff probability $\delta_s$, and receives an offer from $s$ with
probability $\pi_s$ if employed or $\pi_s^U$ if unemployed.
The household accepts the offer with the higher value, smoothed by additive
idiosyncratic preference shocks explained later.

The Bellman Equation for each individual $i$ in sector $s\in\{F,I\}$ is
\begin{align*}
    V_t^s(a,e,\beta,\theta) = &\max_{c,h,a'} \Bigg\{
    \frac{c^{1-\frac{1}{\gamma}}}{1-\frac{1}{\gamma}}
    - \psi \frac{h^{1+\frac{1}{\varphi}}}{1+\frac{1}{\varphi}}
    + \beta \E_t \!\bigg[ \delta_s V_{t+1}^U(.)
    + (1-\delta_s) \bigg( \\
    &\quad\qquad \pi_F (1-\pi_I) \max\!\left\{V_{t+1}^s(.,\theta), 
            \E_\theta V_{t+1}^F(.,\theta')\right\} \\
    &\quad\qquad + (1-\pi_F)\pi_I \max\!\left\{V_{t+1}^s(.,\theta), 
            \E_\theta V_{t+1}^I(.,\theta')\right\} \\
    &\quad\qquad + \pi_F \pi_I \max\!\left\{V_{t+1}^s(.,\theta), 
            \E_\theta V_{t+1}^I(.,\theta'),
            \E_\theta V_{t+1}^F(.,\theta')\right\} \\
    &\quad\qquad + (1-\pi_F)(1-\pi_I) V_{t+1}^s(.,\theta)
    \bigg) \bigg] \Bigg\} \\
    \text{s.t.} \qquad &c+a' = (1+r_t^a) a + y_t(s,e,\theta) + T_t b + \tau_t \\
    &a' \in [\underline a, \infty),
\end{align*}
where formal workers take hours as given. And for each unemployed worker is
\begin{align*}
    V_t^U(a,e,\beta) = &\max_{c,a'} \Bigg\{
    \frac{c^{1-\frac{1}{\gamma}}}{1-\frac{1}{\gamma}}
    + \beta \E_t \!\bigg[ (1-\pi_F^U)(1-\pi_I^U) V_{t+1}^U(.) \\
    &\quad\qquad + \pi_F^U (1-\pi_I^U) \max\!\left\{V_{t+1}^U(.),
            \E_\theta V_{t+1}^F(.)\right\} \\
    &\quad\qquad + (1-\pi_F^U)\pi_I^U \max\!\left\{V_{t+1}^U(.), 
            \E_\theta V_{t+1}^I(.)\right\} \\
    &\quad\qquad + \pi_F^U \pi_I^U \max\!\left\{V_{t+1}^U(.), 
            \E_\theta V_{t+1}^I(.), \E_\theta V_{t+1}^F(.)\right\}
    \bigg] \Bigg\} \\
    \text{s.t.} \qquad &c+a' = (1+r_t^a) a + y_t(U) + T_t b + \tau_t \\
    &a' \in [\underline a, \infty).
\end{align*}

As in \textcite{caliendo2019trade}, before deciding, the households receive
an offer $A\in\{F,I,FI\}$ and draws a vector $\{\varepsilon^j\}$ over
the options $j\in\mathcal{S}$, with scale $\sigma_S$\footnote{
    $\sigma_S$ governs the smoothness of the decision. As $\sigma_S \to 0$
    it collapses to the deterministic $\max$.},
and picks the option $j$ maximizing $V^j + \varepsilon^j$.
This delivers a closed-form expected value,
\[
\P^{A}(j) = \frac{\exp\!\left( V^j/\sigma_S \right)}
{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}} \exp\!\left( V^k/\sigma_S \right)},
\qquad
\E\!\left[ \max_{j\in\mathcal{S}} \left\{ V^j + \varepsilon^j \right\} \right]
= \sigma_S \ln \sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}
    \exp\!\left( \frac{V^j}{\sigma_S V_a} \right)\footnote{
    $\sigma_S$ is scaled by the marginal value of wealth, $V_a$, so that the
    randomness of the sectoral choice does not vary mechanically with wealth.
    We use a standard linear weighted approximation for the log-exp sum.}.
\]

These shocks render the transition a differentiable function,
which allows it to be linearized when computing the sequence-space Jacobians,
as in \textcite{iskhakov2017endogenous}. Therefore, the sector transition
matrix $\Pi_t^s = \P(s'|s)$ is given by
\[
\Pi_t^s = \begin{bmatrix}
    (1-\delta_F) \phi_F(F) & (1-\delta_F) \phi_F(I) & \delta_F \\
    (1-\delta_I) \phi_I(F) & (1-\delta_I) \phi_I(I) & \delta_I \\
    \phi_U(F) & \phi_U(I) & \phi_U(U)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where $\phi_s(s')$ is the probability of accepting an offer $s'$ given that
the worker is in sector $s$,
\begin{align*}
\phi_F(I) &= \pi_F \pi_I \, \P^{FI}(I) + (1 - \pi_F) \pi_I \, \P^{I}(I),
\qquad \, \; \phi_F(F) = 1 - \phi_F(I), \\
\phi_I(F) &= \pi_F \pi_I \, \P^{FI}(F) + \pi_F (1 - \pi_I) \, \P^{F}(F),
\qquad \phi_I(I) = 1 - \phi_I(F), \\
\phi_U(F) &= \pi_F^U \pi_I^U \, \P^{FI}(F) + \pi_F^U (1 - \pi_I^U) \,\P^{F}(F), \\
\phi_U(I) &= \pi_F^U \pi_I^U \, \P^{FI}(I) + (1-\pi_F^U) \pi_I^U \, \P^{I}(I),
\qquad \phi_U(U) = 1 - \phi_U(F) - \phi_U(I).
\end{align*}

We denominate the steady-state size of each sector $s\in\{F,I,U\}$ as
$\alpha_s$, such that $\alpha_F+\alpha_I+\alpha_U=1$.
Let $\Lambda_t(s,b,\theta,\beta,e,a)$ be the household distribution over a given
period of time.

%---------------------------------------------------
%	FIRMS
\subsubsection*{Firms}
In the firm side, there is a representative final-good producer that aggregates
a continuum of $j\in[0,1]$ intermediate inputs produced by formal firms,
\[
Y_t = \left(
    \int_0^1 y_{j,t}^\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon} \: dj 
\right)^\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1},
\]
where $\varepsilon>0$ is a constant substitution elasticity (CES).

Each intermediate good $j$ is produced by a monopolistic competitive
firm $y_{j,t} = Z_{t} l_{j,t} $. They hire labor at real wage $w_t$ and set
a constant markup of $\mu = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}$.
Hence, $P_t = \mu W_t/Z_t$, and real wage equals $w_t = Z_t/\mu$ at every date
in steady-state. There is a mass $1$ of outstanding firm shares with
end-of-period price $p_t^e$ \parencite{auclert2025fiscal}.

We assume sticky prices following \textcite{rotemberg1982monopolistic},
standard in Neo-Keynesian's literature, with a quadratic adjustment cost
\[
\Psi_t(\pi_t) = \frac{\mu}{\mu-1} \frac{1}{2\kappa}
\left[ \log (1+\pi_t) \right]^2 Y_t.
\]

Firms' dividends are taxed at rate $\tau_d$, so
$d_t = (1-\tau_d) (Y_t - w_t L_t) - \Psi_t(\pi_t)$, where $L_t = \E[l_{j,t}]$
is the aggregate formal labor. Such as \textcite{hagedorn2019fiscal},
the Phillips curve for price inflation is given by
\[
\ln(1+\pi_t) = \kappa \left[
\frac{w_t}{Z_t} - \frac{1}{\mu}
\right] + \frac{\ln(1+\pi_{t+1})}{1+r_{t+1}} \cdot \frac{Y_{t+1}}{Y_t},
\]

Additionally, there is a representative informal firm operating in a perfectly
competitive market and producing under constant returns to scale,
so $Y_t^I = w_t L_t^I$.

%---------------------------------------------------
%	UNIONS
\subsubsection*{Unions}
Nominal wages are assumed to be sticky as in \textcite{erceg2000optimal},
where unions set nominal wages to maximize agent utility subject to
\textcite{rotemberg1982monopolistic}'s adjustment costs.
We assume that unions allocate all formal labor hours uniformly across agents,
so $ h_{i,t} = h_t $ \parencite{auclert2025fiscal}.

Also, as in \textcite{hagedorn2019fiscal}, union's objective is to maximize
the utility of an agent with average consumption $\bar C_t = \int_0^1 c_{it}
\: di$, discounted by the average discount factor $\bar \beta$, considering
only formal workers \parencite{auclert2021exchange}. This results in a
Phillips curve for wage inflation $\pi_t^w = (1+\pi_t) w_t / w_{t-1} $ given by
\[
\ln (1+\pi_t^w)
= \kappa_w \left[ \psi \cdot h_t^{1/\varphi}
- \frac{(1-\tau_\ell) w_t}{\mu_w F_t} \int_F e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t} u'(c_{i,t}) d\Lambda_t \right]
+ \bar \beta \ln (1+\pi_{t+1}^w).
\]

The Phillips Curve calculations for price and wage are found in the Appendix.

%---------------------------------------------------
%	GOVERNMENT
\subsubsection*{Government}
Monetary Policy follows a standard lagged Taylor Rule, without microfoundations
and only with an inflation target, $i_t = r^*_t + \phi \pi_t$, where $r^*$
is the neutral real interest rate, $i_t$ is the nominal interest
rates, $pi_t$ is the inflation, and $\phi > 1$ \parencite{taylor1993follow}.
Let $1+r_t = (1+i_{t-1})/(1+\pi_t)$ be the real interest rates.

In the governmental sphere, we have a government that collects taxes, makes
transfers, and incurs debt; therefore, the government's budget constraint
is characterized by
\begin{align*}
    \tau_t + T_t \cdot \text{BF}_t + (1+r_t) B_{t-1}
    &= B_t + \tau_\ell w_t L_t + \tau_d (Y_t - w_t L_t) \\
    &= B_t + \tau_d Y_t - (\tau_d - \tau_\ell) w_t L_t.
\end{align*}

We consider two types of government policy, a revenue-neutral and a deficit-financed.
The policymaker chooses a path of the conditional transfers and adjusts transfers
or debt such that the budget is balanced at each period.

The revenue-neutral policy sets a path of $T_t$ and adjusts transfers such that
\[
\tau_t = \tau_d Y_t - (\tau_d - \tau_\ell) w_t L_t - T_t \cdot \text{BF}_t - r_t \bar B,
\]
which means that the fiscal policy is passive and the monetary policy is active.
The nominal interest rate adjust at least one-for-one to changes in inflation.

However, governments normally finance themselves by increasing deficit and
issuing new debt. The deficit-financed policy sets a path of $T_t$ and $\tau_t$,
adjusting the debt to pay off. We assume that the government repays its debt
in the long run by reducing transfer (when $\tau_t > 0$) or increasing taxes
(when $\tau_t < 0$), with elasticity $\phi_B$,
\[
\tau_t = \bar \tau - \phi_B \cdot (B_{t-1} - \bar B).
\]

Or, in another words, using \textcite{auclert2025fiscal} notation, the debt
persistency is defined by
\[
dB_t = (1+r_t) dB_{t-1} + d\tau_t = (1 + r - \phi_B) dB_{t-1} = \rho_B dB_{t-1},
\]
where $\rho_B$ is the debt persistency, which we calibrate so that debt has a
half-life of 5 years, so $\rho_B = {0.5}^{1/20} \approx 0.966$ and $\phi_B = \mPhiB$.

This situation leads to larger multipliers and great inequality
\parencite{auclert2023trickling}. In the presence of nominal rigidity and
non-Ricardian consumer behavior, \textcite{angeletos2024can} show that fiscal
deficits of this sort self finance, so $\lim_{t\to\infty} B_t = \bar B$.

Finally, there is a mutual fund collecting household savings $a_{i,t}$ and
investing on government bonds and the stock market. No-arbitrage condition
garanties that
\[
(1+r_{t+1}) p_t^e = p_{t+1}^e + (1-\tau_d) d_{t+1} \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad
p_t^e = \sum_{s\geq 1} \frac{(1-\tau_d) d_{t+s}}{\prod_{k=1}^s (1+r_{t+k})},
\]
which implies
\[
1+r_t^a = \frac{(1+r_t) B_{t-1} + d_t + p_t^e}{B_{t-1} + p_{t-1}^e} = 1+r_t,
\quad \forall t\geq 1.
\]

At $t=0$ the shock is unanticipated, so households hold a portfolio bought at
steady-state prices. The initial return $r_0^a$ is such that the liquidation value
of the mutual fund equals the beginning-of-period value of its stock and bond holdings,
\[
(1+r_0^a) A = p_0^e + d_0 + (1+r_0) B,
\]
so $1+r_0^a$ is a weighted average of the initial stock return $(p_0^e+d_0)/p^e$
and the bond return $1+r_0$, with weights given by the steady-state portfolio shares
$p^e/A$ and $B/A$. The shock thus revalues household wealth on impact through both
assets, while every Euler equation prices the ex-ante return $r_{t+1}^a = r_{t+1}$.

%---------------------------------------------------
%	MKT CLEARING
\subsubsection*{Market Clearing}
Finally, the asset, labor, and goods markets reach equilibrium
(Market Clearing Conditions). First, the asset market reaches
equilibrium when household savings equal stocks and government bonds
\[
A_t = \int a_{i,t} \; d\Lambda_t(s,b,\theta,\beta,e,a) = B_t + p_t^e;
\]
the labor market clears when the share of employment in the workforce equals
the firm's demand for labor
\begin{align*}
    &L_t = \int \mathbbm{1}\{s_{i,t}=F \} \cdot \theta^F_{i,t} \cdot
            e_{i,t} \cdot h_{i,t} \; d\Lambda_t(.); \\
    &L_t^I = \int \mathbbm{1}\{s_{i,t}=I \} \cdot \theta^I_{i,t} \cdot
            e_{i,t} \cdot h_{i,t} \; d\Lambda_t(.);
\end{align*}
and the goods market clear when $Y + Y^I = C + \Psi_t$. We set the goods market to
clear by Walras' Law. Lastly, the BF benefit is given to all eligible households,
\[
\text{BF}_t = \int b_{i,t} \; d\Lambda_t(.).
\]

\textit{\textbf{Equilibrium.}} Given initial conditions $(B_{-1}, p_{-1}^e, \Lambda_0)$
and exogenous paths $\{T_t, r^*_t\}_{t\geq0}$, an equilibrium is a sequence of prices
$\{w_t, \pi_t, \pi^w_t, i_t, r_t, r^a_t, p^e_t\}$, macroeconomic aggregates
$\{Y_t, Y^I_t, L_t, L^I_t, h_t, d_t, \tau_t, B_t, C_t, A_t, \text{BF}_t\}$, transition
matrices $\{\Pi_t\}$, household policies $\{c_t, a_t, h^I_t\}$ over the state
$(s, b, \theta, \beta, e, a)$, and distributions $\{\Lambda_t\}$ such that, for all $t\geq0$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The policies solve the Bellman equations of each sector;
    \item Firms price according to the Price Phillips curve and the union sets wages
    according to the Wage Phillips curve, with $h_{i,t} = h_t$ for every formal worker;
    \item The government satisfies its budget constraint and fiscal rule, and monetary
    policy follows the Taylor rule, with $1+r_t = (1+i_{t-1})/(1+\pi_t)$; The mutual
    fund prices stocks by no-arbitrage and pays $r^a_t$;
    \item $A_t = B_t + p^e_t$, $L_t$ and $L^I_t$ equal the effective labor
    supplied by formal and informal workers, $\text{BF}_t = \int b \; d\Lambda_t$, and
    $Y_t + Y^I_t = C_t + \Psi_t$ holds by Walras' law.
\end{enumerate}

A steady state is an equilibrium in which prices and aggregates are constant, $\Lambda$
is invariant, and $\Pi$ is a fixed point. Numerically, the transition solves for
$\{B_t, L_t, h_t, \pi_t, w_t, \tau_t, p^e_t\}$ such that the fiscal rule, the
asset and labor markets, both Phillips curves, the government budget and the
no-arbitrage condition hold.

\subsubsection*{Sectoral Transitions}
The transition matrix across $s\in\{F,I,U\}$ is estimated on the PNAD-C rotating panel,
which follows each household for five consecutive quarters. Two issues arise before the
matrix can be taken to the model.

First, quarter-to-quarter transitions are contaminated by classification error: a worker
whose contract status is misreported in one interview appears as two spurious moves. The
error is detectable because a Markov chain must satisfy $P(h)=P(1)^{h}$. Table
xxx compares the matrix estimated directly at horizons of one to four
quarters with the iterated one-quarter matrix. At one year the quarterly matrix predicts
$\P(F\to I)=0.251$, while the same panel measures $0.116$; the same gap appears for
$\P(I\to F)$, $0.334$ against $0.155$. Iterating $P(1)$ compounds the measurement error,
so the one-quarter matrix overstates mobility by a factor of two.

We therefore estimate the transition matrix over one year, from the first to the fifth
visit, and convert it to the quarterly frequency by its principal fourth root,
\[
    \Pi_s = P(4)^{1/4},
\]
computed by eigendecomposition. The root is a valid transition matrix here: all entries
are non-negative and the rows sum to one, so no regularization of the generator is needed.
The resulting quarterly flows are $\P(F\to I)=\mDatFI$ and $\P(F\to U)=\mDatFU$, against
$0.084$ and $0.012$ in the raw quarterly panel. The cost of this choice is that round
trips within the year are not observed, so $\Pi_s$ is a lower bound on true mobility;
we prefer it to a matrix whose implied one-year flows are twice what the data show.

Second, panel attrition is selective: households that stay in the sample for five visits
are more likely to be formally employed. The stationary distribution of the raw matrix is
$(0.549,\,0.415,\,0.036)$ against the cross-sectional shares $(\mDatF,\,\mDatI,\,\mDatU)$.
We correct it by scaling the columns of $P$ by a vector $c$ and renormalizing the rows,
\[
    \tilde P_{ss'} = \frac{P_{ss'}\,c_{s'}}{\sum_{k} P_{sk}\,c_{k}},
\]
where $c$ solves the fixed point that makes the stationary distribution of $\tilde P$ equal
to the annual stocks $\alpha$, normalized at $c_F=1$. The tilt reweights the destinations,
not the origins, so it preserves the ranking of the flows out of each state; it moves
$\P(F\to I)$ by less than one percentage point.

\subsubsection*{Job Tenure}
The tenure question (V4040, with V40401 to V40403) gives the time the worker has been in
the current job, which disciplines how often jobs end. Restricting to private employees
with a formal contract (VD4009 $=01$), since public employment has a stability the model
does not represent, mean tenure is $\mDatTen$ months and a share $\mDatTenOneTwo$ of workers
have been less than one year in the job. Under a constant exit rate $\eta$ and stationary
hiring, tenure in the stock of employed workers is geometric, so
\[
    \Pr(\text{tenure}<1\ \text{year})=1-(1-\eta)^{4}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \eta = 1-\left(1-\Pr(\text{tenure}<1\ \text{year})\right)^{1/4} = \mDatSep .
\]
The share with less than three months of tenure, $0.070$, implies the same rate, which
supports the constant-hazard approximation. In the model the job exit rate is
$\eta=\delta_F+(1-\delta_F)\,\phi$, where $\phi$ is the probability that an employed formal
worker receives and accepts an outside offer. Since $\delta_F$ and the sectoral flows are
matched by construction, the residual identifies the formal-to-formal job change, which
the sectoral flows cannot see, and it is what we use to calibrate the taste dispersion
$\sigma_S$. Note that this discipline is only available once the quarterly flows come from
the annual matrix: with the raw quarterly flows the sectoral exit rate alone, $0.097$, already
exceeds the job exit rate $\mDatSep$, which is impossible since every sectoral move is also
a job change.

\subsection*{Methods}

The main computational methods are the Sequence-Space Jacobian (SSJ)
\parencite{auclert2021using}, and the Endogenous Grid Method (EGM)
\parencite{carroll2006method}. The EGM was combined with the discrete choice
among sectors using \textcite{iskhakov2017endogenous}, with taste shocks.

The central idea of SSJ is to summarize the entire model by a matrix equation
$\mathbf{F}_t(\textbf{X},\textbf{Z}) = \textbf{0}$, where $\textbf{X}$ are the
endogenous variables and $\textbf{Z}$ are the exogenous ones. The Implicit
Function Theorem allows us to differentiate this system and obtain General
Equilibrium Jacobians, written as
\[
d\textbf{X} = \textbf{G} \, d\textbf{Z}
= -\textbf{F}_\textbf{X}^{-1} \textbf{F}_\textbf{Z} \, d\textbf{Z}.
\]

For each shock in \textit{Bolsa Família}, we can compute the Jacobian
$dC = \textbf{G}_{C,T} \, dT$ of the change in the conditional transfer
on household consumption. The Jacobian is computed by perturbing the
exogenous variable $T$ and solving the model for the new equilibrium,
then calculating the change in consumption $C$.

Further, we can compute the consumption response to a monetary shock
$dC = \textbf{G}_{C,i} \, di$ in the presence of the transfer, and compare
it to the counterfactual. We measure the stabilization effect of the transfer
as the difference between the response in the economy with the program and
in the $T=0$ counterfactual,
\[
\text{Stab}(T) = dC \, \big|_{T>0} - dC \, \big|_{T=0},
\]
read either as an impulse response or as the induced change in the volatility of
consumption $\Delta \!\Var(C_t)$, a program that smooths fluctuations delivers
$\Delta \!\Var(C_t) < 0$. This effect can be decomposed in a insurance and
a composition channel,
\[
d\textbf{X} = d\textbf{X}^\text{ins}
+ \left( d\textbf{X} - d\textbf{X}^\text{ins} \right)
= \textbf{G}^\text{ins} \, d\textbf{Z}
+ \left( \textbf{G} - \textbf{G}^\text{ins} \right) d\textbf{Z}.
\]

The sectoral block of the transition matrix $\Pi_t$ is endogenous and depends on
the continuation values, $\Pi_t = \Pi(\textbf{V}_t, \Lambda_t)$, where
$\textbf{V}_t = (V_t^F, V_t^I, V_t^U)$. The household's distribution evolves by
combining the asset policy with the Markov transition over the states,
$\Lambda_{t+1} = \mathcal{T} \!\left( \Pi_t, a_t(s,\theta,\beta,e) \right)$.
To solve the steady-state distribution, we iterate until $\Pi_t$ converges
to its fixed point, analogue to \textcite{aguirregabiria2007sequential}.

The insurance channel is captured by the frozen-$\Pi$. Holding sectoral sorting
fixed, the transfer relaxes the budget of unemployed and low-income informal
households, and, since these are high-MPC, it dampens the aggregate
response \parencite{auclert2024intertemporal}.

The composition channel is captured by the moving-$\Pi$ Jacobian, because the CCT
raises the value of informality relative to formal work, so workers are tilted
toward the informal sector, which is riskier. The insurance term is stabilizing,
while the composition is potentially destabilizing. The sign and magnitude of the
net effect are the quantitative answer to our question.

Letting the transition respond, $d\Pi_t = \Pi_{\textbf{V}} \, d\textbf{V}_t$,
yields the general equilibrium Jacobian $\textbf{G}$. This dynamic is
obtained as the dynamic analogue of the steady-state: given a shock path, we
iterate so that households' sectoral choices are consistent with the values
those very choices generate along the transition \parencite{auclert2021using}.

\subsection*{Calibration}

Each model period stands for one quarter and the computational time horizon is
100 quarters (or 25 years). We internally calibrate the steady state by setting
$\beta_H$ to balance the asset market; $\pi_F$, $\pi_I$, $\pi_{UF}$, $\pi_{UI}$ to
match the sectoral transition; and $\lambda$, $\bar y$, $\rho_F$ to match the BF
coverage by sector in steady state (Figure \ref{tab:bf_coverage}). Also, we
internally calibrate parameters using the Simulated Method of Moments (SMM) by
a Global Search on a coarse grid, followed by a local polish using the
Nelder-Mead algorithm. The external calibration is shown in table
\ref{tab:ext_calibration} and the endogenous calibration is in
\ref{tab:int_calibration}.

The idiosyncratic productivity follows a log-normal $AR(1)$ process,
$\ln e' = \rho_e \ln e + \varepsilon^e$, with $\varepsilon^e \sim N(0,\sigma^2_e)$.
This is approximated by a finite Markov chain with $N_e = \mNE$ grid points
\parencite{rouwenhorst1995asset}. We internally calibrate $\rho_e$ and $\sigma_e$
to match the autocorrelation of wages in PNAD-C
$\rho_4^y := \text{Corr}(\ln y_t, \ln y_{t-4})$ and the observed wage distribution.

We also assume a log-spaced grid for assets $[\mAmin, \mAmax]$ with $N_a = \mNA$
points, sufficient to cover the bottom and the top of distribution, and a
log-normal sector productivity grid $\ln \theta^s \sim N(\mu_s, \sigma_s^2)$,
that is discretized by Equiprobable Bins with $N_\theta = \mNT$ nodes.

Each household has a $q = \mQ$ probability of drawing a new discount factor
$(\beta_L, \beta_H)$, corresponding to an average type duration of 25 years,
used to capture a generation \parencite{auclert2025fiscal}.
This aims to capture similar results in relation to a life-cycle
overlapping generations with permanent ability shock model.

In the household preferences, elasticity of intertemporal substitution is standard
in literature $\gamma=\mEis$\footnote{
    EIS $\gamma=\mEis$ is consistent with micro estimates surveyed by
    \textcite{valickova2015financial}.}.
The disutility of labor $\psi = \mPsi$ is calibrated to normalize formal
hours $h_F = \mHF$ in steady state.
% At last, $\varphi = \mVarphi$ is chosen to approximate
% informal worked hours to PNAD-C Data, in average (model = $0.891$, data = $0.867$)
% and in standard error (model = $0.35$, data = $0.34$).
Finally, the preference shock smoothing parameter is set to $\sigma_S = \mSig$.

The patient's discount factor $\beta_H = \mBetaHigh$, balances the asset market
in steady state. We estimate the impatient's discount factor, $\beta_L = \mBetaLow$,
and the share of impatient households, $\omega_I = \mOmegaI$, to match the Gini
Coefficient on Wealth ($\mDatGini$, from UBS Global Report) and the income-weighted
Marginal Propensity of Consumption (MPC)\footnote{
    Income-Weighted MPC is consistent with the estimates of \textcite{costa2025fiscal}
    and \textcite{galveasuncertain}.
} ($\mDatMpc$), letting the Top 10\% Share of Wealth
($\mDatTopOneZero$, from World Inequality Database) and the share of
Hand-to-Mouth households ($\mDatHtm$) untargeted.

The layoff probabilities are equal to the transition probabilities in PNAD-C,
$\delta_F = \mDeltaF$ and $\delta_I = \mDeltaI$. Further, the job offer
probabilities are calibrated to match sector sizes $s\in\{F,I,U\}$ in
equilibrium, and their transition, as shown in Table \ref{tab:transitions}.

\input{transition_table.tex}

We internally calibrate moments of $\theta$ to match three quantiles
$\{0.25, 0.50, 0.75\}$ of the observed wage distribution for formal and
informal workers, with respect to $\E(y^F)$, which yields $\mu_I = \mMuI$,
$\sigma_F = \mSigmaF$, and $\sigma_I = \mSigmaI$, after normalizing
$\E\!\left[\theta^F_{i,t}\right] = 1$.

The average wage is normalized $w=\mW$, so that we can reference the model as a
percentage of the Wage Bill. That was prefered rather than normalizing
the GDP, because there is no capital in this model, therefore the wage if a better
measure of the economy's size. We defined $\mu = \mMu$, which is standard in
the HANK literature \parencite{kaplan2018monetary,auclert2025fiscal}\footnote{
    Markup $\mu=\mMu$ is also consistent with the estimates of
    \textcite{basu1997returns} for US. \textcite{de2021quantifying} show that
    the Brazilian markup is similar to the US.},
and $\kappa = \kappa_w = \mKappa$, similar to \textcite{kaplan2018monetary}
and consistent with an average duration of 4 quarters for readjustment\footnote{
    We calculate $\kappa$ following the standard Calvo formula
    $\kappa = \frac{1}{1+\Gamma} \frac{(1+\theta)(1-\bar \beta \theta)}{\theta}$, with
    the real rigidity coefficient $\Gamma = 5$ and the quarterly wage adjustment
    probability $1 - \theta = 25.4\%$ from Caccioli (2026).
}.

Taylor Rule is pretty standard, with $\phi=\mPhi$ and $r^* = \mRstar$, consistent
with a $5\%$ annual real interest rate for Brazilian economy
\parencite{dos2024natural}. Also, we set $\phi_B = \mPhiB$ so that the half-live
of the debt is 5 years, slower than \textcite{auclert2025fiscal} and consistent
with \textcite{campos2019alert}.

Wage tax is based on the average formal worker contribution, $\tau_\ell = \mTauL$,
and profit's tax is based on the statutory rates for Brazil, IRPJ and CSLL, which
are 25\% and 9\% respectively. The Government debt is set to $\mDatBGdp$ of yearly
GDP, consistent with Brazilian National Treasure data. The steady state stock
market size is $p^e = d/r = \mPE$, so $A/Y = \mModAGdp$, compared to $\mDatAGdp$
from BCB Credit Statistics for liquid wealth.

We calibrated the \textit{Bolsa Família} transfer size $T = \mTr$ to match the
\textit{Bolsa Família} expenditure as a percentage of the Wage Bill,
$\text{BF} \cdot T / W$ from Brazilian economy. In order to keep the internal
consistency of the model, we consider $T$ as the average transfer received only
by indivuals in labor force, disconsidering children and elderly.

On the other hand, the entry probability $\lambda = \mLambdaBF$, the threshold
$\bar y / y^F = \mYbar$, and the audit probability for formals $\rho_F = \mRhoF$ are
internally calibrated to match the coverage by sector, $BF_U = \mDatBFU$,
$BF_I = \mDatBFI$, and $BF_F = \mDatBFF$, where $BF_s := \P[b=1|s]$.
The audit probability for informals $\rho_I = \mRhoI$ is set by a $2$ years time
for review, standard in CadÚnico. $\bar y$ is consistent with the $35th$ percentile
of formal income \parencite{ferriere2025heterogeneous}, $y_{35} / y^F = \mDatYThreeFive$,
and $\rho_F$ is consistent with an average time of $\mModTBFF$ months to lose the benefit.

\subsubsection*{Price Phillips Curve}

Let $Y_t$ be the aggregate output of the economy, a CES of the intermediary inputs,
\[
Y_t = \left(
    \int_0^1 y_{j,t}^\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon} \: dj 
\right)^\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}.
\]

This firm maximizes its output subject to $\int_0^1 p_{i,j} y_{i,j} \; dj$.
The FOC implies that the demand for the intermediate good is given by
\[
y_{j,t} = \left( \frac{p_{j,t}}{P_t} \right)^{-\varepsilon} Y_t,
\]
where $P_t$ is the equilibrium price of the final good,
\[
P_t = \left( \int_0^1 p_{j,t}^{1-\varepsilon} \; dj \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}.
\]

We assume a \textcite{rotemberg1982monopolistic} quadratic price
adjustment cost given by
\[
\frac{\theta_p}{2} \left( \frac{p_{j,t}}{p_{j,t-1}} - \bar \Pi \right)^2 Y_t,
\]
where $\theta_p = (\varepsilon-1) / \kappa$ is the adjustment cost multiplier
and $\bar \Pi$ the steady-state inflation.

Let $\Xi(y)$ be the intermediary firm's cost. Given last period's price $p_{j,t-1}$,
the firm chooses this period's price $p_{j,t}$ to maximize the present discounted
values of future profits,
\[
V_t^{IF}(p_{j,t-1}) = \max_{p_{j,t}} \frac{p_{j,t}}{P_t} y_{j,t} - \Xi(y_{j,t})
- \frac{\theta_p}{2} \left( \frac{p_{j,t}}{p_{j,t-1}} - \bar \Pi \right)^2 Y_t
+ \frac{1}{1+r_{t+1}} V_{t+1}^{IF} (p_{j,t}).
\]

We can replace the demand function into this problem, so
\[
V_t(p_{j,t-1}) = \max_{p_{j,t}} \frac{p_{j,t}}{P_t}
\left( \frac{p_{j,t}}{P_t} \right)^{-\varepsilon} Y_t - \Xi(y_{j,t})
- \frac{\theta_p}{2} \left( \frac{p_{j,t}}{p_{j,t-1}} - \bar \Pi \right)^2 Y_t
+ \frac{1}{1+r_{t+1}} V_{t+1} (p_{j,t}).
\]

The FOC with respect to $p_{j,t}$ is
\[
(1-\varepsilon) \left( \frac{p_{j,t}}{P_t} \right)^{-\varepsilon} \frac{Y_t}{P_t}
+ \varepsilon mc_{j,t} - \theta_p \left( \frac{p_{j,t}}{p_{j,t-1}} - \bar \Pi \right)
\frac{Y_t}{p_{j,t}} + \frac{1}{1+r_{t+1}} V'_{t+1}(p_{j,t}) = 0,
\]
where $mc_t = \partial \Xi / \partial y_{j,t}$, and the envelope condition gives us
\[
V_{t+1}' = \theta_p \left( \frac{p_{j,t+1}}{p_{j,t}} - \bar \Pi \right)
\frac{p_{j,t+1}}{p_{j,t}} \frac{Y_{t+1}}{p_{j,t}}.
\]

Thus, taking $p_{j,t} = P_t$ in equilibrium,
\[
\theta_p (\pi_t - \bar \Pi) \pi_t
= \varepsilon \left( mc_t - \frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon} \right)
+ \frac{1}{1+r_{t+1}} \theta_p (\pi_{t+1} - \bar \Pi) \pi_{t+1} \frac{Y_{t+1}}{Y_t},
\]
where we define the firm's markup as $\mu = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}$.
Linearizing around the steady-state $\pi_t = \bar \Pi$,
\[
\ln (1+\pi_t)
= \frac{\varepsilon}{\theta_p} \left( mc_t - \frac{1}{\mu} \right)
+ \frac{1}{1+r_{t+1}}\ln (1+\pi_{t+1}) \frac{Y_{t+1}}{Y_t},
\]
where $\kappa = \varepsilon / \theta_p$ and $mc_t = w_t / Z_t$. This environment
is equivalent to \textcite{calvo1983staggered} in first order around zero inflation.

\subsubsection*{Wage Phillips Curve}

We will use the same setting as \textcite{hagedorn2019fiscal}, but considering just
the formal sector. Assume there is a union that aggregate effective labor input into
\[
h_t = \left(
    \int_0^1 e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t} (h_{i,t})^\frac{\varepsilon_w-1}{\varepsilon_w} \: di 
\right)^\frac{\varepsilon_w}{\varepsilon_w-1},
\]
sells households labor services $e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t} h_{i,t}$ at nominal wage $W_{i,t}$
to the labor recruiter, which minimizes costs
$\int_0^1 W_{i,t} e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t} h_{i,t} \; di$, implying a demand of
\[
h_{i,t} = \left( \frac{W_{i,t}}{W_t} \right)^{-\varepsilon_w} h_t.
\]

The unions sets a nominal wage $\hat W_t$ for an effect unit of labor,
$W_{i,t} = \hat W_t$ to maximize profits subject to
\textcite{rotemberg1982monopolistic}'s wage adjustment costs
\[
e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t} \frac{\theta_w}{2}
\left( \frac{\hat W_{t}}{\hat W_{t-1}} - \bar \Pi^w \right)^2 h_t.
\]
We assume that the union's wage setting problem is to maximize the
aggregate utility of its members, so
\[
V_t^w = \max_{\hat W_t} \int \left[
u'(c_{i,t}) \frac{(1-\tau_\ell)\hat W_t}{P_t} e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t} h_{i,t}
- v(h_{i,t}) - e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t} h_t \frac{\theta_w}{2}
\left( \frac{\hat W_t}{\hat W_{t-1}} - \bar \Pi^w \right)^2
\right] d\Lambda_t + \bar \beta V_{t+1}^w.
\]

Using the same algebra as the price Phillips curve, assuming that the union
allocates the same amount of hours in each worker $h_{i,t} = h_t$, and using
$\int_F d\Lambda_t = F_t$, the size of the formal sector,
\[
\theta_w (\pi_t^w - \bar \Pi^w) \pi_t^w
= \varepsilon_w \left[ v'(h_t)
- \frac{\varepsilon_w-1}{\varepsilon_w} (1-\tau_\ell) w_t \Lambda_t^F \right]
+ \bar \beta \theta_w (\pi_{t+1}^w - \bar \Pi^w) \pi_{t+1}^w \frac{h_{t+1}}{h_t},
\]
where $\Lambda_t^F = \int_F e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t} u'(c_{i,t}) d\Lambda_t / F_t$.
We can linearize around the steady state $\pi_t^w = \bar \Pi^w$ and take
$h_{t+1} = h_t$, since there is no population nor technological growth. Thus,
\[
\ln (1+\pi_t^w)
= \frac{\varepsilon_w}{\theta_w}
\left[ v'(h_t) - (1-\tau_\ell) w_t \Lambda_t^F
\frac{\varepsilon_w-1}{\varepsilon_w} \right] + \bar \beta \ln (1+\pi_{t+1}^w).
\]

Therefore, defining $\kappa_w = (\varepsilon_w/\theta_w)$ at the steady state
and $\mu_w = \varepsilon_w/(\varepsilon_w-1)$, the wage Phillips curve is
\[
\ln (1+\pi_t^w)
= \kappa_w \left[ \psi \cdot h_t^{1/\varphi}
- \frac{(1-\tau_\ell) w_t}{\mu_w F_t} \int_F e_{i,t} \theta_{i,t} u'(c_{i,t}) d\Lambda_t \right]
+ \bar \beta \ln (1+\pi_{t+1}^w).
\]
